A small business owner posted on Reddit last month that a customer had shamed them for using AI to design a flyer. The replies did not go the way you would expect.

The top comment, with over a hundred upvotes, said roughly this: if it looked like AI junk, that is the problem. If nobody would have known unless you told them, it was fine.

That distinction now decides whether AI for small business helps you or costs you. Nobody is angry that you used a model. They are angry that they can tell.

## The mood turned faster than most owners noticed

The numbers moved a long way in twelve months. In 2025, around one in five consumers said heavy AI use would lower their trust in a brand. This year that figure is closer to two in five. Half of US consumers now say they would rather buy from a business that keeps generative AI out of its customer-facing content.

Gen Z is sharper still. Around seven in ten say they have taken action against a brand after spotting AI marketing: unfollowed, unsubscribed, complained publicly, or walked away from a purchase they were going to make.

A year ago, AI in your marketing was a talking point. Now it is a liability the moment it shows.

## Production became free, so production stopped counting

Look at what the model makers shipped this summer. Google released Gemini 3.7 Flash on 13 August at half the price of the model it replaced, and it beats the previous Pro tier on most benchmarks. Gemini now generates over 150 million images a day. OpenAI, Anthropic and Perplexity all moved in the same direction in the same weeks, each cheaper and faster than the version it replaced.

I wrote in June that [the price of intelligence keeps falling while your bill goes up](/writing/ai-is-getting-cheaper-your-bill-is-going-up). The other half of that story is what it does to your marketing. When producing a competent newsletter costs nothing, nobody credits you for one.

Every firm on your high street can now generate a passable logo, a serviceable blog post, a tidy customer email. Competent-looking no longer signals effort or care, because it no longer takes any.

## What is actually scarce now

The scarce thing is judgement: looking at what the model produced and knowing whether it is any good.

A model will hand you the average of everything ever published about your industry. It is very good at this. The trouble is that average is what your customers are drowning in, and they have got quick at spotting it. The tell is the absence of a real opinion.

This is the same lesson I keep arriving at from different directions. [The model was never the hard part](/writing/the-real-money-in-ai-is-implementation-not-models). The hard part is what you know about your own customers.

## You have more room here than the big brands do

One finding in the research should cheer you up. Customers cut local and small businesses more slack on AI than they cut national brands, because they understand the resource constraint. A supermarket chain using AI in its ads reads as cost-cutting. A two-person firm using AI reads as resourcefulness.

You also hold something a national brand cannot buy. You know your customers by name. You have opinions about your trade that you formed by doing it. That is the raw material the models do not have, and it is sitting unused in most small businesses.

## A worked example

A recruitment firm I worked with had their consultants drafting candidate summaries with a model. Response rates from clients fell. When they asked why, clients told them every summary now read the same.

We kept the model and changed what went into it. Instead of "summarise this CV", the consultant fed in their own notes from the interview call, including the blunt off-hand judgements they would never have typed into a formal document. Would struggle in a large team. Clearly bored where they are. Worth meeting even though the CV looks thin.

The model handled structure and speed. The consultant supplied the opinion. Response rates recovered within a month and then went past where they had been before, because those summaries now contained something no rival firm's summaries contained.

Same model, same tool. The input changed from public information to private judgement, and that was the fix.

## On whether to tell people

Owners worry about disclosure more than they need to. The research on this is calmer than the headlines. Clear disclosure mostly protects trust or leaves it flat, and about three quarters of younger consumers say knowing makes them either more likely to buy or no less likely.

Being caught damages trust. Being open does not, and the work itself is what gives you away.

So the rule is short. Never publish anything you would be embarrassed to see labelled.

## What to do this month

Pull up your last ten customer-facing pieces. Emails, posts, proposals, quotes. Read them as a customer would. Mark every line that could have been written about any firm in your sector. That is your slop, and it is costing you more than the subscription does.

Then take your most repeated customer-facing job and write down the judgement that belongs in it. The read you have on a client. The thing you always check that competitors miss. The opinion you would give a friend. Feed that in as the input and let the model do structure and speed, which is what it is good at.

Last, pick one place to be plainly human: a founder note, a real photo, or a view you are prepared to defend. One piece that could only have come from you does more for trust than ten polished ones that could have come from anywhere.

The tools got cheap enough that everyone can produce. Nobody can copy what you think. [That is how you stay irreplaceable](/writing/ai-wont-take-your-job) in a market where the output is free and the judgement is not.
